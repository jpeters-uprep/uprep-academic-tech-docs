The practical side of using Fusion for class work — access, files, units, and
handoff. For how the software itself works, see
[Fusion Training](/makers/fusion-training), which curates Autodesk's own video
lessons.

## Getting access

Fusion is free for you as a student through Autodesk's education program. Full
instructions are on the
[Autodesk software page](/software/fully-supported/autodesk) — follow those
rather than the steps a random tutorial gives you, since the education sign-up
path is specific to schools.

Two things worth knowing up front:

- **Sign in with your `@universityprep.org` email.** If you use a personal
  Autodesk account, your files won't be where you expect them next class and
  you'll lose the education license.
- **The education license renews annually.** If Fusion suddenly asks you to
  subscribe partway through the year, that's the renewal, not a broken install —
  re-verify through the education page.

## Where your files live

Fusion saves to Autodesk's cloud by default, not to your laptop. That's a good
thing here: your files follow you between your laptop and the shop computer, and
you can't lose a project to a dead hard drive the night before a milestone.

- **Save often, and give the save a comment.** Every save creates a version you
  can go back to. That comment ("added cable notch," "shrank tabs to 3mm") is
  what makes the version history useful three weeks later.
- **Don't rely on the local recovery cache.** If Fusion crashes before you've
  ever saved, there may be nothing to recover. Save as soon as you have a
  sketch, not once you're done.
- **Name files so someone else can find them.** Include the project and your
  name or team — `vr-shelf-team3-jpeters` beats `Untitled (17)`. This matters
  most when you're handing a file off for machine time.

## Units

Fusion lets you set the unit type per design, and mixing them up is one of the
most common ways a part comes out at the wrong scale.

- **3D printing is a millimeter world.** Bambu Studio, filament diameters, and
  nozzle sizes are all specified in millimeters.
- **Plywood and hardware are sold in inches.** A "1/2 inch" sheet is nominally
  12.7 mm — and, as [Designing for the CNC](../cnc-design-rules) covers, it's
  usually not actually that thick.
- **Pick one unit for a design and stay in it.** If you need to work across
  both, measure in the unit your material is sold in and let Fusion do the
  conversion, rather than converting in your head partway through a sketch.

## Handing a file to someone else

Which format you send depends on what the other person needs to do with it — see
[Exporting Your Design](../exporting-your-design) for the full table. The short
version:

- **A teammate who needs to keep modeling it** — send the Fusion design itself
  (share the cloud project, or export an `.f3d`). Never send an STL for this; an
  STL throws away your sketches, parameters, and history, and there's no way to
  get them back.
- **A teammate or June who just needs to check dimensions or cut it** — a STEP
  file, or the DXF/3MF the machine actually needs.
