Everything on this page follows from one fact: **the cutting bit is a spinning
cylinder.** It removes material in a circle, so any shape it cuts inherits the
roundness of that circle. Designs that ignore this look fine in Fusion and fail
on the machine.

Read this while you're still in CAD. Fixing these problems after the toolpath is
made means going back to CAD anyway.

## A round bit cannot cut a sharp inside corner

If you draw a square pocket and cut it with a 1/4 inch (6.35 mm) bit, the inside
corners come out with a 3.18 mm radius — half the bit diameter. The bit
physically cannot reach into the corner.

This matters most when a square tab has to seat into that pocket: the tab's
sharp corners hit the pocket's round corners and the joint won't close.

Three ways to handle it:

- **Design the radius in.** Round the inside corners of your pocket to the bit
  radius or larger, and round the mating part's corners to match. Cleanest
  result, and the part looks intentional.
- **Add relief cuts — dogbones or T-bones.** A small extra circle or slot cut
  into each corner gives the tab's corner somewhere to go. Standard practice for
  finger joints and slot-together assemblies. Slightly visible; completely
  functional.
- **Sand or chisel the corners by hand.** Fine for one or two corners, tedious
  for twenty.

Outside corners are the opposite: a bit cuts those perfectly sharp, no allowance
needed.

## Minimum feature size

- **No hole, slot, or gap smaller than the bit diameter.** A 3 mm slot cannot be
  cut with a 6.35 mm bit at all — the software will silently skip it, or cut
  something wider than you drew.
- **Keep thin walls thicker than the bit.** A 2 mm-wide standing rib in plywood
  will splinter off during the cut even if the geometry is technically valid.
- **Avoid tiny islands.** A small piece surrounded by cut material has nothing
  holding it and becomes a projectile. If your design needs one, keep it
  attached with tabs.

## Tabs: keep your part from moving mid-cut

When a through-cut finishes, the part is a loose piece inside a spinning cutter.
Tabs are small uncut bridges that hold it to the surrounding stock until you cut
or snap them free afterward.

- Put tabs on straight edges, not on corners or on a surface that has to end up
  perfectly flat.
- More small tabs beat a few thick ones — easier to clean up, same holding
  power.
- Plan where they'll go while you're designing, so they don't land in the middle
  of a joint face.

## Model to the real thickness of the material

A sheet sold as 1/2 inch plywood is usually not 12.7 mm, and two sheets from the
same rack can differ. If you model a 12.7 mm dado and the sheet is 11.6 mm, the
shelf rattles.

**Measure the actual sheet with calipers, then model that number.** Better
still, drive the dimension from a
[Fusion parameter](https://www.autodesk.com/support/technical/article/caas/sfdcarticles/sfdcarticles/How-to-find-a-Parameters-dialog-box-in-Fusion-360.html)
named something like `stockThickness`, so switching to a different sheet is a
one-number edit rather than a remodel.

See [Tolerance and Fit](../tolerance-and-fit) for how much clearance to leave
once you have the real measurement.

## Depth, and why one pass isn't enough

The machine cuts through thick material in several shallow passes rather than
one deep plunge. A pass that's too deep is how bits break and edges burn. The
toolpath software handles this for you once you've picked the right bit and
material. Carbide 3D's own
[feeds and speeds guidance](https://carbide3d.com/hub/docs/) is the source to
check before you invent your own numbers, and ask June if you're unsure what to
run for a given bit and material.

What this means for your design: **depth costs time.** A pocket 3 mm deep cuts
in a fraction of the time of one 12 mm deep. With one CNC shared across the
class, shallow-where-possible is a real design consideration, not just an
optimization.

## Leave room for work holding

The stock has to be held down, and the bit cannot cut where a clamp is sitting.
Leave a border of waste material around your parts — generally an inch or so —
and don't nest parts right up to the edge of the sheet.

Clamps and screws into the wasteboard are the usual answer. Double-stick tape
also works when it suits the part, and it has the advantage of leaving the whole
top surface clear. Which one to use depends on the cut, so talk it through
before you commit a layout that only works one way.

## Plywood-specific gotchas

- **Cut direction affects tearout.** The top veneer can splinter along the cut
  edge. It shows most on cross-grain cuts and on the face you'll see.
- **Voids happen.** Cheap plywood has internal gaps you can't see until you cut
  through one. Not your fault; plan a spare if a visible face matters.
- **Sanding changes dimensions.** If you plan to sand a joint face, you're
  removing material after you cut to size. Account for it or sand only the faces
  that don't have to fit anything.

## Watch out for topping out

Our machine stops counting position once the Z axis runs to the top of its
travel. If a retract move sends it up that far, the machine loses track of where
Z actually is, and the next plunge cuts deeper than your toolpath says it
should. Keep retract heights inside the machine's real travel — see
[Running the CNC](../cnc-workflow) for the check to run before you start a job.
