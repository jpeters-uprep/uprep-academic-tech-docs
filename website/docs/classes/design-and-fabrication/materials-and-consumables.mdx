Choosing material is a design decision, not a supply-closet errand. The same
bracket in PLA, in PETG, and in plywood behaves differently, and picking wrong
usually shows up as a broken part rather than a wrong-looking one.

**What's actually on the shelf changes.** Check with June
(jpeters@universityprep.org) before you commit a design to a material you
haven't seen in the room, and well before you need a quantity of it.

## Filament

| Material | Behaves like                                    | Reach for it when                          | Trade-off                                     |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| PLA      | Stiff, hard, slightly brittle                   | Most parts, prototypes, anything cosmetic  | Softens in a hot car; snaps rather than bends |
| PETG     | Tougher, slightly flexible, more heat-resistant | Parts that flex, get handled, or take load | Stringier; slightly fussier to print well     |
| TPU      | Rubbery and flexible                            | Bumpers, grips, gaskets                    | Slow to print; not for structure              |

PLA is the right default for this class — it prints reliably and it's what
you'll iterate in. Move to PETG when a part has failed in PLA for a reason you
can name, not preemptively. Bambu Lab's
[filament guide](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/general/filament-guide-material-table)
covers what each material needs from the printer, and their
[PLA](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament/pla) and
[PETG](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament/petg) pages go deeper.

Colors are whatever is loaded. If a specific color matters to your design, ask
early rather than assuming.

## Sheet goods for the CNC

- **Plywood** is the workhorse — strong, cuts cleanly, holds a screw. Quality
  varies, and cheap sheets have internal voids you'll only find by cutting into
  one.
- **MDF** is flat, uniform, and cheap, with no grain to tear out. It makes the
  worst dust in the shop (mask up, see [PPE](/makers/ppe)), swells if it gets
  wet, and holds screws poorly in its edges.
- **Plastic sheet** cuts well with the right bit and settings. Ask before
  running any plastic you didn't get from the shop — some plastics melt onto the
  bit, and a few release fumes you don't want to be near.

The number that matters is the **measured thickness**, not the label. See
[Designing for the CNC](../cnc-design-rules).

## Paper, card, and cardboard

Underrated, and the fastest material in the building.

- **Cardstock** takes fine detail on the Silhouette and is good for templates,
  patterns, and drilling guides.
- **Corrugated cardboard** is the test-fit material. Cutting a full-size
  cardboard version of a part before it goes on the CNC is the single cheapest
  way to catch a dimension error — see
  [Vinyl and Cardstock Cutting](../vinyl-and-cardstock-cutter).

Use these freely. That's what they're for.

## Vinyl

- **Heat-transfer vinyl (HTV)** for fabric, applied with the heat press. Cut
  mirrored.
- **Adhesive (sticky) vinyl** for hard surfaces — labels, logos, bin markings.
  Applied with transfer tape.

## Hardware

We stock general-purpose fasteners: wood screws, machine screws and nuts,
washers, and heat-set inserts for printed parts. For anything specific — a
particular length, a drawer slide, a hinge, a specialty fastener — assume it has
to be ordered and tell June **weeks** before you need it, not the day your team
reaches that step. An ordered part arriving late is one of the few delays no
amount of good work recovers from.

## Electronics

LEDs, batteries, and basic circuit components are available, and there's some
capacity for microcontrollers if a project genuinely calls for one. Talk to June
before designing electronics into a project so the scope stays realistic
alongside the fabrication work.

## Using material well

- **Prototype in the cheap material.** Cardboard, then scrap plywood, then the
  good sheet. Never let your first attempt at a part be on your only piece of
  nice material.
- **Nest your parts.** Lay out cuts to use the sheet efficiently rather than
  cutting one part out of the middle.
- **Keep your offcuts.** The scrap bin is where test coupons and second attempts
  come from — for you and for everyone else.
- **Label your material.** An unlabeled half-sheet on the rack becomes shared
  scrap.
