Machines cut parts. Hand tools turn parts into an object. Almost every build in
this class ends with measuring, drilling, clamping, gluing, and driving screws —
and a build that was cut precisely can still come out crooked if it's assembled
carelessly.

The full inventory of what we have lives on
[Makers Tools](/makers/makers-tools#hand-tools). This page is about using it.

## Measuring

**Calipers are the tool that makes your CAD match reality.** Almost every fit
problem in this class starts with a dimension somebody guessed.

- Use calipers for anything that has to fit something else — material thickness,
  a bolt shaft, the inside of a cabinet, a printed part you're matching.
- Measure the same thing twice in two places. Plywood is not uniform, and a
  sheet can be 11.4 mm at one corner and 11.9 mm at another.
- Write the measurement down when you take it. A number you remember is a number
  you'll get wrong.
- Use a tape measure for long spans, and read it against the same edge every
  time — the hook on the end slides on purpose.

## Drilling

- **Clamp the work.** A part that spins when the bit catches is how hands get
  hurt. This applies to a hand drill, not just the drill press.
- **Match the bit to the job.** A hole for a screw shaft to pass through is
  bigger than a hole for a screw to bite into. Two different holes, two
  different bits.
- **Back up the exit side** with a scrap board to keep the far face from blowing
  out.
- **Bare hands near the drill press.** No gloves, no loose sleeves — see
  [PPE](/makers/ppe).

## Clamping and glue

- **Dry-fit the whole assembly before any glue touches it.** Glue-up is a timed
  event and it's the worst moment to discover a part is backwards.
- **Clamp squarely, and check with a square** rather than by eye. A cabinet
  glued out of square stays out of square.
- **Glue is stronger than the wood on a long-grain joint** and weak on an end-
  grain butt joint — which is why the joints below exist.
- Wipe squeeze-out while it's wet. Dried glue won't take finish and is miserable
  to sand off.

## The joints we use

Unit 2 deliberately varies the joint type across teams, so you'll see several of
these in the room at once.

| Joint              | How it works                                          | Good for                        | Watch out for                                                                 |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Butt joint         | Two square edges meeting, held by screws or glue      | Fast, low-load assemblies       | Weakest option; end-grain glue barely holds                                   |
| Rabbet             | A step cut along one edge, the other part sits in it  | Box corners, back panels        | Needs accurate depth or the corner shifts                                     |
| Dado               | A slot across one part, the other slides in           | Shelves in a case               | Slot must match measured thickness, not nominal                               |
| Finger / box joint | Interlocking square fingers                           | Strong CNC-cut box corners      | Inside corners need relief — see [Designing for the CNC](../cnc-design-rules) |
| Dovetail           | Angled interlocking fingers that resist pulling apart | Drawers, anything under tension | Least forgiving of dimension errors                                           |
| Mortise and tenon  | A peg on one part into a socket in the other          | Frames, legs, structural joints | Requires careful layout on both parts                                         |

Every one of these depends on the slot matching the tab. Get the number from
[Tolerance and Fit](../tolerance-and-fit) and from a test cut, not from the
nominal thickness printed on the sheet.

## Fastening printed parts to wood

This comes up constantly once your project mixes 3D-printed parts with CNC-cut
structure.

- **Screws straight into plastic** work for light loads, but the threads chew
  out after a few removals. Fine for something assembled once.
- **Heat-set inserts** are brass threads pressed into a printed hole with a
  soldering iron. They give you a real machine-screw thread that survives being
  taken apart repeatedly. Worth it for anything serviceable.
- **Bolt through, nut behind** is the simplest strong option when you can reach
  both sides — model a hex pocket to capture the nut and it becomes a one-wrench
  job.
- **Design the fastener in from the start.** A hole added after the fact usually
  lands somewhere structurally unfortunate. If your project requires real
  fasteners, they belong in the CAD model.
