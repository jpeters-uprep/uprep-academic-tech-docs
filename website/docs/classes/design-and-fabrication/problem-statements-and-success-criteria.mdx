You've [interviewed someone](../user-interviews) and you have pages of notes.
This is the step where those notes become something you can design against — and
the step most teams skip, which is why they end up building the first idea
somebody said out loud.

Two artifacts come out of it: a **problem statement** (what needs solving, and
for whom) and **success criteria** (how you'll know if you did).

## The problem statement

One or two sentences. It names the user, the need, and why it matters — and it
contains **no solution**.

A usable shape:

```text
[User] needs [the need] because [what's true about their situation].
```

The test: **could two teams read your problem statement and build genuinely
different things?** If not, you've written a build order, not a problem
statement.

Here's one that passes:

> The coach needs to move gear between fields quickly, because he sets up on
> three fields a week and currently carries it in armloads.

That names the user, the need, and the evidence behind it, and it leaves the
form of the solution wide open — a cart, a bag, a sled, a rack that clips onto
something he already pushes around. Any of those could be the right answer.

These don't pass:

| Not a problem statement                          | Why it fails                             |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| "Build a three-tier rolling cart with dividers." | It's a solution. Nothing left to design. |
| "Storage is bad."                                | No user, no need, nothing to aim at.     |
| "The coach needs a better gear caddy."           | "Better" isn't a need. Better how?       |

Things to check before you commit to yours:

- **Is the need in the user's terms or yours?** "Needs a modular system" is your
  vocabulary. "Needs to find the right ball without unpacking everything" is
  theirs.
- **Is it the underlying need or the surface complaint?** See
  [User Interviews](../user-interviews).
- **Is there evidence behind it?** You should be able to point at a sentence in
  your notes.
- **Is it one problem?** If yours has an "and" joining two unrelated needs, you
  probably have two problems and should pick.

## Constraints are not criteria

Keep these in a separate list. A constraint is something that's simply true and
not up for negotiation — you don't get points for meeting it, you fail if you
don't.

- Physical limits: it must fit in a space this size, weigh under this much.
- Existing conditions: it has to attach to something that already exists, in a
  place you can't drill.
- Access: a specific person has to be able to reach or lift it.
- Shop reality: it has to be cuttable on our machines, out of material we have,
  in the class periods that remain.

Write them down explicitly, with numbers, and **measure the real thing** rather
than trusting a stated dimension. Every unwritten constraint is a redesign
waiting for you.

## Success criteria

These are the checks you'll run on the finished object. Good ones are
**testable, specific, and written before you build** — so they can't be quietly
rewritten to match whatever you managed to make.

Each criterion should answer: _what will I do to test this, and what result
counts as passing?_

| Weak criterion      | Testable version                                                                            |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "It's easy to use." | "The user can take out one item and put it back without moving anything else."              |
| "It's sturdy."      | "It holds 15 lb on the top surface with no visible flex."                                   |
| "It's organized."   | "Every item has one labeled home, and nothing has to be stacked."                           |
| "It looks good."    | "The user picks it over the current setup when asked which they'd rather keep in the room." |
| "It fits."          | "It slides into the cabinet with the door closing fully, with clearance for the cable."     |

Aim for four to six. Fewer and you're not covering the need; more and you're
writing a specification instead of a design target.

Mix in:

- **At least one criterion the user gave you**, in something close to their own
  words.
- **At least one you can measure with a tool** — a tape measure, a scale, a
  stopwatch.
- **At least one about use, not appearance.** Something someone has to do with
  it.

## Keep them visible, and check them twice

Success criteria that live in a notebook nobody reopens are decoration. Put
yours somewhere your team sees them while working.

Then check against them **twice**:

- **At your follow-up check-in**, mid-project. Ask your user whether these are
  still the right criteria. Sometimes they aren't, and that's a legitimate
  reason to revise them — as long as you write down what changed and why.
- **On the finished build.** Run each test and record the result honestly,
  including the ones you didn't pass. A build that misses two criteria and knows
  exactly which two is a better piece of engineering work than one that claims
  to have met all six without testing any.

A criterion you failed is not a bad grade waiting to happen — it's the most
useful sentence in your write-up, because it's the one that tells you what to do
next.
