Our CNC is a Shapeoko 3 XL — a computer-controlled router. You design a toolpath
on a computer, the machine drives a spinning bit through material to match it.
It is the loudest, dustiest, and least forgiving machine in the shop, and the
one with the longest queue, so preparation matters more here than anywhere else.

**You must be certified before running it without an adult standing with you.**
See [Machine Certification](../machine-certification).

Carbide 3D — who make the machine and the software — publish a complete free
course,
[Running a Shapeoko](https://carbide3d.com/hub/courses/running-shapeoko/). Work
through it rather than treating this page as a substitute; this page covers our
machine, our sequence, and our shop rules.

## Before you touch the machine

- **PPE.** Safety glasses always. Hearing protection while it's cutting — for
  you and for anyone standing nearby. An N95 for MDF and heavy dust. Details on
  the [PPE page](/makers/ppe).
- **No gloves.** The router spins. Gloves near a spinning tool are the one
  exception-free rule in the shop.
- **Tie back anything loose** — hair, sleeves, drawstrings, lanyards.
- **Have your design finished and exported.** DXF, correct units, closed
  contours. See [Exporting Your Design](../exporting-your-design). Machine time
  is not CAD time.

## Software: two programs, two jobs

| Program            | What it does                                                                |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Carbide Create** | Turns your 2D outlines into toolpaths and exports the file the machine runs |
| **Carbide Motion** | Drives the machine — jogging, zeroing, starting the job                     |

Both are free from Carbide 3D
([Carbide Create](https://carbide3d.com/carbidecreate/download/),
[Carbide Motion](https://carbide3d.com/carbidemotion/download/)) and installed
on the shop computer. Carbide 3D's
[Carbide Create course](https://guides.carbide3d.com/create/) is the
walkthrough; we won't duplicate it.

## Making toolpaths in Carbide Create

1. **Set the stock size and thickness** to the actual piece of material you're
   going to clamp down — measured, not nominal.
2. **Import your DXF** and check one known dimension against your CAD model. If
   it's off, the units are wrong. Fix it now.
3. **Set the zero corner** to match where you'll zero on the real machine.
   Getting these two out of sync is the most common way a job cuts into a clamp.
4. **Pick your bit from the tool library**, and pick the one actually in the
   machine.
5. **Create the toolpaths** — contour for outlines, pocket for recesses, and
   choose whether the cut runs inside, outside, or on the line. Cutting on the
   line makes every part half a bit-width too small; this trips up nearly
   everyone once.
6. **Add tabs** to any part that gets cut all the way through.
7. **Simulate.** Watch the whole preview. Look for cuts going where you didn't
   intend, features that vanished because they're smaller than the bit, and
   plunges deeper than your stock.
8. **Save the file** with a version number in the name.

## At the machine

1. **Secure the stock.** Clamps or screws into the wasteboard, with the border
   you left in your design. Wiggle it — if you can move it by hand, the router
   can move it at speed.
2. **Install the bit** and tighten the collet properly. A bit that slips mid-cut
   plunges deeper as it goes.
3. **Initialize the machine** in Carbide Motion and let it home itself. Keep
   hands clear; it moves on its own.
4. **Set zero** — X, Y, and Z — to the same corner you chose in Carbide Create.
   Carbide 3D's
   [Movements and Zeroing](https://carbide3d.com/hub/courses/running-shapeoko/movements-zeroing/)
   lesson covers this in detail. Double-check Z: a Z zero that's off by the
   thickness of your material is how a bit gets driven into the wasteboard.
5. **Run an air pass first if you're unsure** — raise Z well above the stock and
   run the job so you can watch the path move without cutting anything.
6. **Turn on dust collection**, then start the job.
7. **Stay with the machine the entire time.** Hand on the stop, eyes on the cut.
   Never walk away from a running job — not for a minute, not to grab something.

## Stop the machine if

- The sound changes — a new pitch, chattering, or a stall.
- You see smoke or smell burning.
- The stock shifts, or a clamp moves.
- The bit is cutting somewhere you didn't expect.
- You're not sure. Stopping a job costs a few minutes; the alternatives cost
  more.

Get an adult before restarting after any of these.

## After the job

1. Let the spindle stop completely before reaching in.
2. Retrieve your parts, then cut or snap the tabs and clean up the edges.
3. **Vacuum the machine and the floor around it.** The next person is queued
   behind you and shouldn't inherit your chips.
4. Return the bit to its case and log anything odd — a dull bit, a loose clamp,
   a strange noise — to June (jpeters@universityprep.org) so the next team
   doesn't rediscover it the hard way.

## Planning around the queue

There is one CNC and a whole class. Two habits keep this from becoming your
bottleneck:

- **Get your CAD and toolpaths finished before your machine slot**, not during
  it.
- **Cut a cardboard test template first** on the
  [Silhouette](../vinyl-and-cardstock-cutter). It costs minutes instead of an
  hour and catches the mistakes that would otherwise cost you a second slot.
