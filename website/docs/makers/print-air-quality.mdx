3D printers melt plastic, and melting plastic gives off ultrafine particles and
volatile organic compounds. You mostly can't see or smell it, which is exactly
why we handle it by procedure rather than by how the air seems to you at the
time.

Our printers live inside **enclosure tents** that contain those emissions and
vent them. The tents only work if they're closed, and the air inside one needs
time to clear before you put your head in it.

## The rules

1. **The tent stays closed while anything in it is printing.** No propping it
   open to watch, no reaching in through a gap.
2. **Before you open a tent, no printer inside it may be running.** If someone
   else's job is going, pause it remotely — don't open the tent to press a
   button on the machine.
3. **Wait ten full minutes with everything stopped** before you open up. This is
   the air-out.
4. **Use the timer hanging on the tent.** It's there so nobody has to estimate.
   Ten minutes by the timer, not ten minutes by feel — guessing is how this rule
   quietly becomes a five-minute rule.
5. **Open, take your print, and zip the tent back up.** Don't leave it open
   while you clean up your part at the bench.
6. **Restart any prints you paused.** Zip the tent first, then resume. Whoever's
   job you paused is relying on you to put it back.

That's the whole procedure, and the order matters: pause, wait with the timer,
open, remove, close, resume.

## Why ten minutes

Emissions are highest while the nozzle is hot and extruding, and they don't
disappear the instant a print finishes — they hang in the enclosed volume until
it clears. Ten minutes with nothing running is what we use to let that happen
before anyone breathes it.

Material matters too. PLA is the mildest of what we run; other filaments emit
more, and some emit noticeably more. That's part of why
[Materials and Consumables](/classes/design-and-fabrication/materials-and-consumables)
asks you to check before running something that isn't PLA, and why the tent
procedure doesn't get relaxed for a "quick" print in a different material.

## If something's wrong

Tell June (jpeters@universityprep.org) if a tent is torn or won't close, a timer
is missing or dead, or you can smell plastic strongly in the room. A tent that
doesn't seal isn't doing its job, and it's not something to work around quietly.

## Further reading

NIOSH — part of the CDC — published a guide written specifically for spaces like
ours:
[Approaches to Safe 3D Printing: A guide for makerspace users, schools, libraries, and small businesses](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2024-103/pdfs/2024-103.pdf)
(publication 2024-103). It's the reference behind how we do this, and it's
readable — worth a look if you want to know why rather than just what.
